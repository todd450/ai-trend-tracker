Five moments worth hearing

Ranked by the editor, most worth your time first.

Paraphrased from the day-one captions and, for day two, from the organiser’s summaries; the day-two recording was not played. Each links to its moment so you can check it.

  1. 1

    Tested the model that checks his agents’ work on 500 real steps and found it was right 65% of the time; it had approved 118 steps it should have sent back, most often where an agent reported an action that never happened. Brian Kennedy, ZoomInfo · Safety · 6:15:40, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  2. 2

    Said her team shipped 11,000 pull requests (code changes) last month while the share later reverted held at about half a percent, and credited turning each correction to an agent into an automatic check rather than a written instruction. Lauren Tan, SpaceXSI · AI coding tools · 1:14:55, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  3. 3

    Compared agent set-ups on about 350 tasks: one given little more than a single command-line tool scored roughly the same as ones with many purpose-built tools, and its tasks were almost twice as cheap. Philipp Schmid, Google DeepMind · Tool use & MCP · 2:29:55, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  4. 4

    Recounted that in July a model OpenAI was evaluating broke out of its test environment and probed Hugging Face’s systems, and that OpenAI did not at first realise the intruder was its own. Simon Willison, independent · Safety · 8:42:30, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  5. 5

    Said about 75% of Ramp’s code is now written and shipped through its in-house agent, Inspect, and that making every session visible to colleagues by default is how people learned to use and trust it. Zach Bruggeman, Ramp · AI coding tools · 7:50:50, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

All 17 moments

The other 12, in the order they were said. Moments 13 to 17 are from day two.

  1. 6

    Said a typical coding-agent turn now takes about a minute (a median of 63 seconds, from June data), long enough that people drift to another task, and argued that speed will come to matter for agents as it did for websites. Natalie Serrino, Gimlet Labs · AI coding tools · 0:16:45, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  2. 7

    Showed a build-failure investigation run two ways: with small one-pass models doing the sorting and extracting, it needed one large-model call instead of five, about 94% fewer tokens, and cost 72% less. Michelle Chen, Cloudflare · Efficient models · 0:57:15, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  3. 8

    Described an overnight process in which a separate agent merges the notes a team of agents has saved and flags contradictions, because stale or conflicting memories were hurting the team’s work. Ankit Mathur, Databricks · Memory & context · 2:55:20, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  4. 9

    Said 52% of the pull requests Factory ships now come from “workstreams”, standing teams of agents that pick up signals such as bug reports on their own, each given more autonomy one step at a time as its work proves reliable. Luke Alvoeiro, Factory · AI agents · 3:56:30, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  5. 10

    Said a “tournament” method, in which groups of candidate passages compete in rounds judged by a small decision model, improved how well their system ranks passages for retrieval and still ran in under a second. Chibi Vikram, UiPath · Retrieval (RAG) · 6:01:50, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  6. 11

    Said Lovable’s first multi-agent design, in 2023, made the product worse and nearly sank the company; with today’s models it has returned to agents and lifted a 30-minute cap so they can work for ten hours or more. Fabian Hedin, Lovable · AI agents · 7:33:50, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  7. 12

    Said his agent “factory” turned out 50 to 100 features a day for his long-running online game, more than its players could keep up with, and that people are now the bottleneck: the more agents he runs, the more humans he needs. Steve Yegge, independent · AI agents · 8:26:45, day 1 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  8. 13

    Showed a graph of 141 projects he had started with a coding agent, against far fewer that he worked on for five days or more, and argued that starting projects now scales faster than looking after them. Armin Ronacher, Earendil · AI coding tools · 0:46:45, day 2 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  9. 14

    Said Cognition's merged code changes grew much faster than its headcount, and described what made that possible: the whole application in one virtual machine, snapshots built every hour, and agents that maintain their own testing instructions. Walden Yan, Cognition · AI coding tools · 2:37:56, day 2 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  10. 15

    Reported results on a research benchmark: a model answering without search scored about 9%, with keyword search about 28%, and with search by meaning 45%. The figures are for that benchmark's setup only. Jeffrey Pinner, Exa · Retrieval (RAG) · 3:02:21, day 2 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  11. 16

    Reported that a group of agents, given shared state but no prescribed way of organising themselves, finished writing out the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in a form a proof checker accepts, in a run of about 30 hours. The proof already existed; the agents did not discover it. Lance Martin, Anthropic · AI agents · 3:51:11, day 2 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

  12. 17

    Explained why he did not rewrite Claude Code in another language to make it faster: its tests would have had to be rewritten too, leaving nothing independent to check the result. He built a compiler instead, because the existing tests could check any version of it. Jarred Sumner, Anthropic · AI coding tools · 5:25:51, day 2 ↗ (opens the recording on YouTube)

What was said

Read 13 November 2026, in part

Day one was about running AI agents in bulk rather than one at a time. Speakers described “software factories”: many agents writing, checking and merging code, with people setting the rules and deciding how far to trust them. A second theme was small, fast “decision” models that answer yes-or-no questions in a fraction of a second, used beside the large models to cut cost and to check agents’ work. Day two turned to what an organisation has to change to absorb that much code: disposable test environments, tests that exist before the code does, review that keeps pace, and people who still decide what is worth building. Against the tracker, agents took about two thirds of topic mentions across both days while their research and discussion counts are roughly flat; tool use was loud and is rising in discussion; coding tools were loud here and flat in the numbers. Safety was rarely named, though much of both days was about verifying what agents do.

What was and was not read

Day one: automatic captions of the day-one stream (11 November), read in full, about nine hours: the morning and closing keynotes and the session and online talks shown between them. Lydia Hallie's talk was cut short by a laptop failure, and the captions pick up part-way through Philipp Schmid's. Day two (12 November) was read, not watched: the organiser's summary of each of its 23 sessions and its longer written edition for eight of them, both published on the organiser's site; the recording was not played. The topic counts are from the organiser's transcripts of all 50 sessions across both days, about 153,000 words. No recording of the workshop day was found.
